Mission Walkthrough

One tiny mission, followed through every idea in the primer. Each step has a live panel you can play with. Nothing here is faked: the search, the trajectory and the quantum simulation all run in your browser.

Step 1
The mission

A small probe sits at S and must dock at G, 13 cells to the right. In between is a band of debris with one gap, and an asteroid drifting down through that gap. We want the route that burns the least fuel and never touches anything.

debrisasteroid (moves down over time)start Sgoal G

Each square is one cell. Time moves in steps: in one step the probe moves by its current velocity. Every change of velocity is a burn and costs 1 unit of fuel (Δv) per cell/step of change.

Step 2 · Classical path planning
A* finds a path on a flat map

First, pretend nothing moves and the probe can stop and turn instantly, like a character in a grid game. That's plain shortest path. Run both searches and compare how many cells each has to look at. Click any cell to add or remove debris.

What you just saw. Dijkstra expands outward like a ripple, in order of distance. A* adds a guess ("how far is the goal in a straight line?") to its priority queue key, so it heads for the goal and skips most of the map. Same answer, far less work.

Step 3 · Trajectory plus navigation
Now add momentum and a moving asteroid

Real spacecraft don't stop and turn for free. They drift, and every change of speed costs fuel. The asteroid also moves. So we search over states: position, velocity and time. From each state the probe can coast or burn (up to one unit in x and y per step). This is the state lattice. Press play and watch both plans fly.

Try the deadline slider. With plenty of time, the cheapest plan is to wait for the asteroid to pass, then drift across: 2 units of fuel (one burn to start, one to stop). Tighten the deadline and the planner switches to a sprint that gets past the gap before the asteroid arrives, which costs more fuel. That trade-off between time and fuel is what trajectory planning is about.

Step 4 · QUBO
Shrink it to a quantum-sized problem

A quantum simulator can't hold the problem above. The trajectory search looked at thousands of states, and QAOA needs a qubit per decision. So we shrink the mission until it fits in 8 qubits. The field is now a 3×3 grid with an asteroid in the middle, so the probe goes around it either over the top or along the bottom. The top route passes through thin debris, so it costs extra fuel to dodge.

Each of the 8 edges gets one bit: 1 means "the path uses this edge". Click the edges to build a path and watch how the QUBO scores it. Broken paths still get a score. They just pay a big penalty.

Why penalties? QUBO has no "if invalid, reject". Every rule becomes a term that adds cost when broken: "leave S exactly once", "arrive at G exactly once", "whatever enters a corner must leave it". Each broken rule adds 5. That's more than the fuel difference between the two routes, so a broken path never beats a real one. Out of all 256 bitstrings, only 2 are valid routes.

Step 5 · Qubits and QAOA
Let the quantum circuit look for the best route

Now a real 8-qubit simulation, running in your browser. The state holds 256 amplitudes, one per bitstring. QAOA starts with all 256 equally likely (a random guess finds the best route 0.4% of the time). Then it applies p rounds of two layers: the cost layer (γ) tags each bitstring by its QUBO cost, and the mixer (β) lets probability flow between neighbors. Move the knobs by hand, or press Auto-tune to let a classical optimizer find good angles. That loop is exactly what QAOA does on real hardware.

What to notice. With p = 1 the best route comes out about 7% of the time, 16 times better than guessing, but the slightly worse top route is just as likely. Adding layers pushes the best route above 20%, still far from certain. You never read the whole distribution on real hardware. You measure, get one bitstring per shot, and keep the best valid one you saw.

Step 6
What this example tells us

  • Classical won easily. The state-lattice search solved the 16×9 mission with momentum and a moving asteroid by checking a few thousand states in milliseconds.
  • Quantum had to shrink the problem to 8 qubits and two possible routes, and even then it gives the right answer only some of the time.
  • Every extra decision is another qubit. The simulator doubles its memory each time, so a 4×4 grid is about the limit (see the table in the primer).
  • That's why our research question is "how big is the gap, and how does it grow?" and not "where does quantum win?". This page is a first taste of the benchmark we'll build.
